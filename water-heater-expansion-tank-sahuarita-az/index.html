Water Heater Expansion Tank Guide for Sahuarita, AZ Homes

A water heater that drips from its relief valve every few hours is often not broken. It is a closed system with nowhere for expanding water to go, and a small expansion tank can fix it.

This guide explains why the tank is needed, how to tell if yours has failed, and what installing one involves.

Why heated water needs room

Water expands when it is heated. In an open system, the extra volume pushes back into the municipal supply line, and nobody notices. If your house has a backflow preventer or a pressure reducing valve, that path is blocked, and the extra volume has nowhere to go.

Pressure then rises every time the heater cycles. Pipes, fixtures, and the heater itself take the stress, and the relief valve may open to protect the tank. An expansion tank absorbs the extra volume.

Signs you may need an expansion tank

These are common clues.

  • ✓The relief valve drips or spits at the end of heating cycles.
  • ✓Faucets or pipes bang or hammer when the heater runs.
  • ✓Water pressure at a hose bib reads high, especially after the heater cycles.
  • ✓Toilet fill valves or supply lines fail more often than expected.
  • ✓A new pressure reducing valve or backflow device was recently installed.

How the tank works

An expansion tank is a small steel tank with a rubber diaphragm inside. One side holds air under pressure, and the other connects to the water line. When water expands, it pushes the diaphragm, and the air cushions the pressure.

The tank connects to the cold water line near the heater. Its air pressure must match your home's water pressure. Installers set it with a tire gauge before the tank goes in, and a mismatch makes the tank useless.

Checking your pressure first

A plumber tests your water pressure with a simple gauge on an outside faucet or the heater drain. Homes with pressure above the normal range for fixtures wear out faster. A pressure reducing valve can lower it, and an expansion tank then handles the thermal swing.

If you see no pressure problem but the relief valve still drips, test the valve itself. Our guide to water heater repair explains how a plumber separates a valve fault from an expansion fault.

What installation involves

A plumber shuts off the water and power, drains a little of the system, and cuts a tee into the cold supply line near the heater. The tank mounts on the tee and needs support if it is large. Then the plumber opens the supply, checks the air charge, and looks for leaks.

It is a modest job, but it should be done on a stable, supported connection. A heavy tank hanging from a copper line can fatigue the pipe over time.

Code questions to ask the Town

The Town of Sahuarita says on its website that it is adopting the 2024 International Codes with local amendments. The page does not say which sections apply to expansion tanks, and we do not guess. Permit rules vary, so ask the Town of Sahuarita building safety department what applies to your project.

If you live in Green Valley, which is unincorporated, ask Pima County instead. The right office depends on where your house sits, and the answer saves you from redoing work.

Replacing a heater? Add the tank

If you are replacing a heater, ask your plumber to check for a closed system and add an expansion tank when needed. It is inexpensive compared with the heater and protects the new tank from pressure damage. See water heater installation for how a new unit is set up.

A second tank on an older heater can also stop the dripping valve that sent you looking in the first place.

Sizing the tank

Expansion tanks come in sizes based on the volume of the water heater and the pressure of the system. A small tank suits a standard 40 or 50 gallon heater in many homes, but a larger heater or higher pressure may need a bigger one. Check the label and the manufacturer's chart.

A tank that is too small does not fully absorb the swing, and the relief valve may still drip. A plumber matches the tank to your heater and your measured pressure.

Maintenance for an expansion tank

It is low maintenance, but not no maintenance.

  • ✓Press the air valve once a year. Only air should come out.
  • ✓Check the air pressure with a gauge while the tank is isolated and drained.
  • ✓Look for rust or wetness around the tank and its connections.
  • ✓Replace the tank if the diaphragm has failed or the shell is rusting.
  • ✓Write the install date on the tank so you know its age.

When pressure is the real problem

Water pressure that is too high stresses every fixture and appliance. A pressure reducing valve, set correctly, protects them. If your pressure reads high on a gauge, the fix may start there.

A bad pressure reducing valve can also cause the problem. If pressure creeps up when no water is running, the valve may have failed. A plumber can test it and replace it, and then size the expansion tank to the system.

Where the rules come from

How thermal expansion must be handled in a closed system is a matter of the plumbing code your jurisdiction has adopted, and the exact wording depends on the edition. Ask the Town's building safety department which one applies to you.

Tanks themselves are rated for a maximum working pressure, printed on the shell, and the manufacturer's guidelines say how to install and charge them.

Expansion tank questions from Sahuarita homeowners

Do all water heaters need an expansion tank?

No. Closed systems with a backflow preventer or pressure reducing valve usually do.

How do I know if my tank has failed?

Press the air valve. If water comes out, the diaphragm has failed.

What air pressure should the tank have?

It should match your home's water pressure. A plumber sets it with a gauge.

Where does it go?

On the cold water line near the water heater.

Does the Town of Sahuarita require one?

Permit and code rules vary. Ask the Town's building safety department.
